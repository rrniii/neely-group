Turbulence mixes air within and around clouds, affecting how showers develop and how much rain reaches the ground. These motions are difficult to observe, and their representation remains a challenge for weather models.

**WOEST — Observing the Evolving Structures of Turbulence** is a NERC-funded programme led by Ryan Neely. It brings together NCAS, the universities of Leeds, Reading, Manchester and Oxford, and Imperial College London, working alongside the Met Office's Wessex Convection Experiment. [Read the NCAS project overview](https://ncas.ac.uk/researchers-awarded-1-8m-to-improve-turbulence-in-weather-forecasts/).

## Observing the same atmosphere from several perspectives

The summer 2023 campaign in southern England combined scanning weather and cloud radars with lidar, unmanned aircraft, radiosondes, cloud cameras, surface instruments and measurements from the FAAM Airborne Laboratory. Coordinating these systems allows researchers to examine cloud development alongside the structure and evolution of the atmospheric boundary layer.

The observations address two connected questions: how turbulence changes as air rises into convective clouds, and how conditions closer to the surface influence those clouds. Comparing the measurements with simulations helps assess where models reproduce the observed motions and where improvements are needed.

## Using radar to test model physics

**Published result · August 2026.** A study co-authored by Ryan compared simulated and observed polarimetric radar signals during **two WesCon case days**. It used differential reflectivity — the difference between radar echoes measured with horizontal and vertical polarisation — to examine how the Met Office Unified Model represents raindrops.

The CASIM double-moment microphysics scheme reproduced the observed variation in radar signals, and the height and intensity of differential-reflectivity columns, more closely than the single-moment scheme in these cases. This provides a way to test the model's cloud and precipitation physics beyond comparing rainfall totals. [Read Lo et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1002/qj.70237).

## Reconstructing three-dimensional winds

**Public preprint · August 2026.** Thompson and colleagues, including Lindsay Rhodes and Ryan, combined observations from **five Doppler radars** to reconstruct winds during the 2023 campaign. Their analysis produces a three-dimensional wind grid at 1 km horizontal and 500 m vertical resolution, every 10 minutes.

The work creates opportunities to compare the structure of convective weather with simulations. Its resolution also limits what can be recovered: the strongest updrafts and downdrafts are underestimated compared with aircraft observations. The linked manuscript is a public preprint with an open review record; it is separate from the published result above. [Read the preprint and review record](https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2026-4631).

## Use the observations

The **WOEST collection at the Centre for Environmental Data Analysis (CEDA)** brings together the campaign's ground-based and airborne datasets. Published in 2025, it includes the observations used to evaluate turbulence and convective dynamics across different spatial and temporal scales. Ryan is the collection's principal investigator.
