Particles influence the radiation travelling through the atmosphere and the chemistry within it. Ryan's research combines lidar and satellite observations with aerosol and climate models to identify their sources and test how their effects are represented.

## Where do the particles come from?

An increase in aerosol does not, by itself, reveal its cause. **Neely et al. (2013)** used simulations to distinguish the effects of moderate volcanic eruptions from increased sulphur dioxide emissions in Asia. Comparisons with observations identified the eruptions as the main explanation for increased aerosol **at 20–30 km altitude during 2000–2010**. [Read the source-attribution study](https://doi.org/10.1002/grl.50263).

A different question arises closer to the tropopause, where the Asian summer monsoon transports and confines air. **Neely et al. (2014)** investigated this seasonal aerosol layer using a model of atmospheric circulation, chemistry and particle formation. The results pointed to human emissions as an important source, including emissions from beyond Asia. Volcanic aerosol can also obscure the layer in observations. Together, these studies show why the altitude, transport and period being studied matter when identifying a particle's origin. [Read the Asian tropopause study](https://doi.org/10.1002/2013JD020578).

## Representing volcanic effects in climate models

Volcanic aerosol changes both the energy absorbed by the atmosphere and the surface area available for chemical reactions. **Neely et al. (2016)** developed a consistent treatment of those effects in the Community Earth System Model, allowing the prescribed aerosol's mass and size to vary.

Tests against the 1991 Pinatubo eruption improved the simulated stratospheric temperature response. The scheme also changed the modelled global surface cooling, although climate variability prevented a firm conclusion about the significance of that change. This work connects observations of particles with the physical processes represented in a climate model. [Read the CESM1 model-development paper](https://doi.org/10.5194/gmd-9-2459-2016).

## Reusable volcanic-emissions data

**VolcanEESM**, developed by Ryan and Anja Schmidt, provides volcanic sulphur dioxide emission amounts and locations for Earth system modelling. The public CEDA collection documents **1850–2015**; its individual records provide the dataset versions, coverage and citation information needed for reuse. [Explore VolcanEESM](https://catalogue.ceda.ac.uk/uuid/bfbd5ec825fa422f9a858b14ae7b2a0d/).
