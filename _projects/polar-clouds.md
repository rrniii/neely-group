Clouds influence how much energy reaches and leaves an ice sheet. Their effect depends on cloud height, phase and water content, and on the aerosol particles from which droplets and ice form.

We combine in situ measurements with radar, lidar and other remote sensing observations to investigate these processes. Work at Summit Station in Greenland connects aerosol properties, fog, mixed-phase clouds and the surface energy budget.

## From Summit to the Greenland melt zone

Ryan developed the **Cloud, Aerosol Polarisation and Backscatter Lidar (CAPABL)** as part of the ICECAPS observing programme at Summit. Its polarisation measurements help distinguish cloud phase and the orientation of ice crystals. This instrument development underpins the group's use of radar and lidar to understand mixed-phase clouds. [Read the institutional research profile](https://environment.leeds.ac.uk/see/staff/1447/dr-ryan-neely-iii).

**ICECAPS-ACE** added aerosol measurements and observations of heat exchange near the surface to the existing Summit observatory. The resulting records connect the particles available to form clouds with the energy exchanged between the atmosphere and the ice sheet. [Access the ICECAPS-ACE data collection](https://catalogue.ceda.ac.uk/uuid/f06c6aa727404ca788ee3dd0515ea61a/).

**ICECAPS-MELT** extends these questions into Greenland's percolation zone, where surface meltwater can enter the firn. Its autonomous platform at Raven Camp measures the coupled snow–atmosphere system using renewable power. [Read about ICECAPS-MELT](https://www.icecapsmelt.org/) and our [autonomous observing systems]({{ '/projects/autonomous-observations/' | relative_url }}).

## Observations across scales

Measurements at the surface are only one part of the picture. Tethered-balloon observations allow us to sample aerosol layers below clouds and test how representative surface measurements are of conditions aloft.

## Antarctic clouds at Troll

Ryan contributed to the international team that installed the **Integrated Cloud Observatory at Troll Station** in 2025. Operated by the Norwegian Polar Institute as part of the Troll Observing Network (TONe), the observatory combines scanning cloud radar, depolarisation lidar, a microwave radiometer and automated radiosonde launches. These measurements help investigate clouds, precipitation and atmospheric conditions in Dronning Maud Land. [Read the NCAS field report](https://ncas.ac.uk/observatory-in-antarctica-enables-scientists-to-study-clouds-like-never-before/) and [explore Troll observations on Cloudnet](https://cloudnet.fmi.fi/site/troll).

## Aerosols and the wider climate system

Ryan's research also spans volcanic emissions and stratospheric aerosol, combining lidar observations with climate modelling. The **VolcanEESM** data collection, developed with Anja Schmidt, supplies volcanic sulphur dioxide emissions for Earth system modelling. This wider work connects particle observations with their effects on climate. [Explore VolcanEESM](https://catalogue.ceda.ac.uk/uuid/bfbd5ec825fa422f9a858b14ae7b2a0d/).
